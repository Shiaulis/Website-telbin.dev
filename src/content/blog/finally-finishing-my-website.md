## Years of false starts

Before the era of AI, I attempted many times to make a website for myself. WordPress, site builders, even writing HTML and CSS by hand: every time, I hit two walls. First, as a mobile developer, I constantly had to pick up a whole extra layer of knowledge outside my main focus just to make the site decent. Second, I never felt I critically needed one. My experiments with social media also convinced me there was no demand for my writing, so it felt like wasted effort. Impostor syndrome was often my biggest companion in those journeys.

In 2025, that started to change. AI was on the rise, and I thought it would finally be easy to build a site with prompts like "make personal website, no mistakes." Yet it still took me a month of tinkering and figuring out which platform to use, how to reuse elements, and where to host it.

Eventually, I landed on Astro and Hostinger. I won't go into much detail on why I chose them, but at the time it was a good fit given my close-to-zero knowledge of modern web development. I got a basic version live, and then I didn't touch it for half a year.

## Trying a coding agent in a field I didn't know yet

In October, I came back to my website. This time I tried bringing a coding agent (Claude Code) into the process. Before that, even basic things like keeping the same header across multiple pages had me stuck.

In one evening, I went from a simple starter page to a more sophisticated site with my career timeline and a working blog. Whenever something felt over-complicated, I tried to simplify it until it felt clean. I also asked AI to explain some of the most complex ideas in simpler language, so I could learn along the way.

For the first time, I was applying my engineering judgment to web development and seeing real visual results in minutes instead of weeks. I was pretty happy with how far I got, yet I still put the site back on hold. There was no real urgency, and at that point it was still just for fun.

## A year later, it stopped being a side project

A year later, my website stopped being a side experiment. After being laid off from Pipedrive, I realized this might be my only real opportunity to shift my focus beyond standard iOS development. Suddenly, my website, LinkedIn, and other social media all mattered a lot more.

I started brainstorming what my next career steps could be. For now, the only certain thing I feel is that I want to try working for myself, so presenting myself only as a developer doesn't make much sense anymore.

My own habits had also changed over the year: AI was no longer an experiment, it had become a crucial part of my daily workflow. With both my direction and my toolkit shifting, I sat down to rethink my site.

## Reliability, design, and a clearer message

This time, the site felt like a real product that I could sell in the future. I wanted good design and a reliable setup, while still learning how to reach those goals on the modern web.

I started with reliability. With AI auditing the project, we caught real issues I would have easily missed on my own: a broken confirmation page after submitting the contact form, no proper 404 page, and weak dark-mode text contrast. I also added automated checks that run every time I publish my website so it wouldn't silently break.

After that, I dug into modern ways of communicating what you do on a personal site. In an Instagram reel (sadly, I didn't save the link, so I can't credit the account where I found it), someone highlighted how [Tori Spiegel](https://torispiegel.framer.website) made "how I can help" the main message of her website. I felt I wanted to do the same. I know people don't browse the internet the way they did in the 2000s, yet having one clear corner where someone can quickly understand how I can help makes it worth it. I kept browsing other personal sites for inspiration too, like [Kseniia's website](https://kafrikantova.me/), where I picked up the idea for a clean contacts section.

I also didn't want generic AI styling or mismatched copy across platforms. So I used my own notes as the source of truth for my CV, LinkedIn, and website, and put together a simple visual identity: a custom logo, consistent colors, typography, and a matching LinkedIn banner.

## Noticing how early we still are with AI

Going through this rebuild also made me look around at how other people use AI. Ever since the layoff, it feels like I sort of woke up. I started noticing how AI is slowly but surely getting into other people's lives. At the same time, adoption rates are still really low, and most people either don't use it at all or use it as a simple chatbot for proofreading. I realized the AI expertise I built over the past couple of years could come in handy, and that I can start helping other people step into this new era.

## What comes next

Right now, I keep exploring different areas where I can help others with AI. I also believe helping others build their own websites is a side path I can try. It can be a good starting point to work with some local businesses or bigger companies that are still missing a proper web presence. My website is just one example of the many small learning journeys and experiments I'm running in new fields.

I use my free time to learn and experiment, focusing on both hard and soft skills so I can better understand the market, the demand, and where I can be most helpful.

In the end, I just want to help people build useful things and share what I've learned. At least at this stage, I feel I enjoy doing that.
